# Shrink the PLC plan to one loop

A complete PLC at Work plan is smaller than the stack of forms suggests. It comes down to **one loop that answers the four critical questions for one essential standard**: pick the standard and turn it into a few "I can" targets, agree on what proficient looks like, give a short common formative assessment (CFA), sort students by target into "needs more time / on target / ready for extension," plan who reteaches and extends, then check again. Solution Tree publishes a separate reproducible for nearly every step, so a team using all the official forms would fill in six to nine sheets. Yet the fields that show up on almost every template come to about ten, and one Solution Tree form, *The PLC Dashboard*'s Essential Learning Target Plan, fits a whole cycle on a single page. The research is clear on where the overwhelm comes from. Teachers turn against PLC work when documents face upward, toward administrators and compliance, instead of feeding the team's next decision. They value it when the work changes what they do in class next week. The usual time budget is about 45 minutes a week, and DuFour's own timed data protocol fits inside it. No current tool walks a team through the whole cycle step by step, and no tool lets teachers share a strategy by invitation, tied to the results. That leaves a real opening for a simple, Google-based guide built on one essential standard and three or four meetings. Everything else in the PLC literature (depth-of-knowledge grids, target types, criteria scoring, calendars, roles, administrator reports) should be optional and hidden by default.

A note on the evidence: the descriptions of Solution Tree templates and protocols below come from the reproducible PDFs themselves. The findings on teacher experience, tools and privacy rest mostly on search-result excerpts and vendor or practitioner sources rather than full articles, so treat them as strong signals, not settled findings.

## Four questions are the whole model, and everything else elaborates them

DuFour's framework rests on three big ideas: a focus on learning, a collaborative culture, and a results orientation. Those ideas turn into four questions that drive the team's work. The canonical *Learning by Doing* wording is: **(1) What is it we want each student to learn? (2) How will we know when each student has learned it? (3) How will we respond when a student experiences difficulty? (4) How will we enrich and extend the learning for students who are proficient?** ([LBD Glossary](https://allthingsplc.info/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Terms.pdf)). Spin-off books add a "fifth question" about teaching practice. An AllThingsPLC post reports that DuFour deliberately left it out so teams would not drift back to talking about teaching instead of learning ([AllThingsPLC blog](https://allthingsplc.secure.agroup.com/blog/view/369/what-is-a-model-plc-school)). For an app, that is a useful rule: **every screen should answer one of the four questions**, and anything that does not is an extra.

Each question has a small set of real components behind it. **Essential standards** answer question 1. Teams choose them using Reeves' tests of endurance, leverage, readiness for the next level, and (in *Learning by Doing*) likelihood of appearing on high-stakes tests ([LBD4 Questions to Guide the Work](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_LBD4/questionstoguidetheworkofyourprofessionallearningcommunity_clarifyessentiallearning.pdf)). This choice is normally made **once a year, not once per unit**. The PDSA cycle tool treats essential standards and pacing as the "big picture look for the year" and then runs a Plan-Do-Study-Act loop unit by unit ([Plan Book: The PDSA Cycle](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_CTPBPLC/thepdsacycle.pdf)). The official protocol for choosing them has seven steps and includes a 60-minute vertical-alignment session ([Identifying Essential Standards Protocol](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_CTPBPLC/identifyingessentialstandardsprotocol.pdf)). That is a year-start task, not something a unit plan should make a team redo.

**Unwrapping** turns a standard into learning targets: circle the verbs, underline the nouns, bracket the context, then list the smaller steps ([Unwrapping Standards Protocol](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_CTPBPLC/unwrappingstandardsprotocol.pdf)). The *Learning by Doing* glossary explicitly endorses student-friendly **"I can" statements** that let students "monitor their own work" ([LBD Glossary](https://allthingsplc.info/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Terms.pdf)). Authors differ on how to label targets. Bailey and Jakicic use a simple **know / do** split ([Five-Step Unit-Planning Template](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_CTPBPLC/five-stepunit-planningtemplate.pdf)), while the RTI at Work tools use four types: knowledge, reasoning, performance skill and product ([Essential Standards Unit Plan](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_RTIAWPB/essentialstandardsunitplan.pdf)). Since the experts themselves disagree on the labels, the labels are optional. The targets are not.

**Proficiency** has to be agreed before teaching. The glossary requires teams to agree on criteria and to apply them consistently ([LBD Glossary](https://allthingsplc.info/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Terms.pdf)). The CFA data protocol gives plain examples: "**two out of three correct** on a multiple-choice assessment, or a **level 3 on the rubric**" ([Protocol for Using CFA Data](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_LGRWE/figure5.6protocolforusingcommonformativeassessmentdata.pdf)). A Solution Tree blog stresses that this agreement must happen "up front," before the assessment is given ([Solution Tree Blog](https://www.solutiontree.com/blog/?p=4874)).

**The CFA** is "the lynchpin of the PLC process." It is given by all teachers within a narrow window and scored with shared criteria ([LBD Glossary](https://allthingsplc.info/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Terms.pdf)). The design protocol keeps it small: "short and easy enough to score so that the team can respond quickly," and "**you do not have to assess every learning target**." Teams assess the targets most likely to cause trouble, or the prerequisite ones ([Developing Assessments Protocol](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_CTPBPLC/developingassessmentsprotocol.pdf)). Research quoted in *Learning by Doing* says high-growth schools use common short-cycle assessments "at least every three to six weeks" ([LBD4: Why Use Common Assessments](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_LBD4/whyshouldweusecommonassessments.pdf)). No Solution Tree source sets a length in items or minutes.

**SMART goals** come in two sizes. The canonical *Learning by Doing* example is an annual team goal: "Last year, 85 percent of our students met or exceeded the target score of 3… This year, at least 90 percent…" ([SMART Goal Worksheet: Third-Grade Team](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_LBD4/SMARTgoalworksheetthirdgradeteam.pdf)). Bailey and Jakicic also attach SMART goals to end-of-unit assessments ([PDSA Cycle](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_CTPBPLC/thepdsacycle.pdf)). Either way, DuFour insists the goal measure **student results, not teacher tasks** such as "we will create a new unit" ([DuFour, JSD 2003](https://learningforward.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/dufour244.pdf)).

**Data analysis** happens "as soon as possible" after the CFA. Teams go target by target and student by student, group students by specific error, and have "each teacher share his or her original instructional strategy so that the team can see if one strategy worked better" ([Analyzing CFA Data Protocol](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_CTPBPLC/analyzingcommonformativeassessmentdataprotocol.pdf)). **Responses** sort students into three columns, "Need Additional Time and Support / On Target / Need Extension," each with a list of students and an instructional plan ([Team Response for Mid-Unit Preventions](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_RTIAWPB/teamresponseformidunitpreventions.pdf)). Students are then reassessed with new items or orally. Note that *extension* (going deeper on the essential standard) is not the same as *enrichment* (electives and field trips) ([LBD Glossary](https://allthingsplc.info/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Terms.pdf)).

**Norms, agendas and minutes** form the team's operating layer. Norms are written once, reviewed at the start of each meeting, and revisited twice a year. The official norms protocol lists five areas to cover: decision making, participation, time management, response to conflict and **confidentiality** ([Identifying Team Norms Protocol](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_CTPBPLC/identifyingteamnormsprotocol.pdf)). The Plan Book agenda has three parts. **Focus it** opens with "Where are we in the PDSA cycle?" **Do it** is the work itself. **Review it** closes with action steps and owners, the next meeting date and agenda, and what data to bring ([Team Meeting Agenda Template](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_CTPBPLC/teammeetingagendatemplate.pdf)).

## Forty templates, one ten-field core

Across roughly 40 published templates from Solution Tree, AllThingsPLC and district manuals, the pattern is consistent. **Solution Tree publishes one small reproducible per task**: essential standards chart, unwrapping template, unit plan, CFA plan, data protocol, team response, SMART goal worksheet, norms and agenda. Districts tend to staple these into one unit-plan or meeting-notes form organized under the four questions. The Lebanon High School manual is an example. Its planner covers all four questions on one sheet per unit, and its rubric insists that an intervention be "a different approach and not a repeat of the same activity" ([LHS PLC Manual](https://allthingsplc.info/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/LHS_PLC_Manual-1.pdf)). The most complete single unit document, the Design in Five template, runs **five pages** ([Design in Five Unit Template](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_HDF/designinfiveunittemplate.pdf)). The leanest is *The PLC Dashboard*'s **Essential Learning Target Plan for Teams**, which covers a whole cycle for one target on **one page** in three blocks ([ELT Plan](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_PLCD/figure41essentiallearningtargetplanforteams.pdf)):

- **Before teaching:** target, CFA date, success criteria ("What score = intervention and what score = extension?"), and a "___% proficient by ___" goal.
- **After the CFA:** percent proficient, which students need more time, intervention and extension plans, and "What instructional practices got the best results?"
- **Reflection:** percent proficient after the action plan, whether the team met its goal, and what to change.

That one page is the closest published blueprint for what PLC Launchpad is trying to be.

Counting which fields recur across the templates separates the true core from the elaborations:

| Tier | Fields | How often they appear |
|---|---|---|
| **Core** | Team, members, date; short norms list; essential standard; "I can" learning targets; CFA (which targets, date, proficiency criterion); a "__% proficient by __" goal; students needing more time and support, with the plan; students ready for extension, with the plan; what worked, compared across classrooms; next steps (who, what, by when) | On nearly every relevant template type |
| **Useful second tier** | Prerequisite skills, academic vocabulary, unit start and end dates, reassessment date, a "what we'd keep or change" reflection | On 6 or more templates, but not universal |
| **Optional extras** | DOK levels, knowledge/reasoning/skill/product labels, criteria-scoring grids for choosing standards, item blueprints with counts per target, calibration dates, calendar grids, resources and technology, student-involvement fields, meeting roles, attendance, "questions for an administrator," root-cause analysis, 4–5 band grouping | On 1–3 templates each |

Sources for the tally: [Essential Standards Chart](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_TARTI/essentialstandardschart.pdf), [Data Analysis Protocol](https://allthingsplc.info/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/data_analysis_protocol.pdf), [Sample Data Analysis Protocol](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_HLPLCSI/sampledataanalysisprotocol.pdf), [SMART Goal Worksheet](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_LBD4/SMARTgoalworksheet.pdf), [Unwrapping Template](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_CTPBPLC/unwrappingstandardstemplate.pdf), [Mathematics Unit Planner](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_MUP_PreK-2/figure1.2mathematicsunitplanner.pdf). One caution: most of these templates derive from Solution Tree, which inflates how much they agree with each other.

The filled-in examples are just as telling. Real completed plans are **short bullets and day ranges, not prose**. The Grade 5 history sample lists standards by code, gives one end-of-unit task, pairs each target with an "exit ticket" or "graphic organizer," and sequences the unit as "Day 1 Launch… Days 2–8…" ([Sample Grade 5 History Unit](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_MIH/figure3.12samplegrade5historyunit.pdf)). A filled grade 4 math unit plan fits on two pages: one standard, "When taught: March. Instructional days needed: Sixteen," and five "I can" statements ([Sample Mathematics Unit Plan](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_TARTI/samplemathematicsessentialstandardsunitplan.pdf)). A real team's minutes end with "Homework: Send quiz data to Jacques by Wednesday" ([NMS PLC Manual](https://allthingsplc.info/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/NMSPLCManual.pdf)). An app built for long text boxes is designing for a team that does not exist.

## Paperwork that faces upward kills the work; paperwork that feeds the next step sustains it

The failure practitioners name most often is **"PLC Lite."** DuFour and Reeves describe it as renaming ordinary meetings as PLCs, running book studies that lead to no action, or spending collaborative time on topics with no effect on achievement ([ERIC EJ1091902](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1091902); [Solution Tree Blog](https://www.solutiontree.com/blog/dont-stop-at-plc-lite/)). Solution Tree's test is blunt: accountable teams "change their practice based on what the student data is showing them" ([Solution Tree Blog](https://www.solutiontree.com/blog/dont-stop-at-plc-lite/)). Teachers' complaints line up with this. In BCG's teacher survey, **more than half were dissatisfied** with PLC collaboration, which "often devolves into an administrative staff meeting," yet teachers still wanted protected time to collaborate ([BCG 2015](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2015/education-public-sector-how-to-help-teachers-get-better-together)). Studies name time as the biggest barrier and teacher buy-in as the second ([ERIC EJ1137497](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1137497.pdf)).

The documents teachers feel as busywork are sign-in sheets sold to hold staff "accountable for their attendance" ([TpT listings](https://www.teacherspayteachers.com/browse?search=plc+documentation)), feedback sheets sent to the administrator after every meeting ([Team Feedback Sheet](https://allthingsplc.info/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/TeamFeedbackSheet.pdf)), and data collected for compliance. One practitioner calls the needed shift "from compliance to meaningful action" ([Tackling Data Overload in PLCs](https://dog-smilodon-f7zs.squarespace.com/blog-2-1/tackling-data-overload-in-plcs)). The activities teachers value are agreeing on must-know standards, unwrapping only those, and looking at real student work together. Solution Tree calls unwrapping "a priceless professional development opportunity" ([Solution Tree reproducible](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_EYT/unwrappingthestandardsapricelessprofessionaldevelopmentopportunity.pdf)). Teams also value using CFA results to "get past the standard to the exact skill" students missed ([Solution Tree Blog](https://www.solutiontree.com/blog/formative-assessment-strategies-to-build-plc-interventions/)).

Solution Tree's own advice for getting started is the strongest support for building a minimal app. **Choose one team, identify one essential standard, commit to one structured response cycle, and build one win at a time** rather than redesigning the whole system ([Solution Tree Blog](https://www.solutiontree.com/blog/formative-assessment-strategies-to-build-plc-interventions/)). The design lesson for PLC Launchpad follows directly. A field earns its place only if a later step uses it: the standard becomes the targets, the targets become the CFA, the CFA results become the groups, and the groups become next steps. Fields that exist to show someone else that the team met (attendance, narrative minutes, administrator questions, self-ratings) are what made the "complete" version feel heavy. Even the official tools warn about this. The RTI at Work planning questions ask outright how to "avoid a process that becomes a paperwork nightmare" ([Effective Use of Tier 2 Time](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_RTIAWPB/effectiveuseoftier2time.pdf)).

## Forty-five minutes a week is enough if the agenda carries itself forward

The common norm is a protected weekly block of **about 45 minutes**. DoDEA calls for "sacred" PLC time of at least one meeting a week lasting 45 minutes or more ([DoDEA PLC Guidance](https://content.dodea.edu/teach_learn/ids/product_showcase/docs/guides/professional_learning_communities_guidance_final_02072017.pdf)). Some schools carve out shorter blocks, such as 38 minutes made by trimming class periods ([Scheduling Practices for PLCs](https://www.wfbschools.com/cms_files/resources/Scheduling%20Practices%20for%20Professional%20Learning%20Communities.pdf)). Others meet every other week ([district slide deck](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1tU2Fsp-_rL0Zy8pQADgBARCeCp05rtkT/htmlpresent)). The official data meeting fits this window. Bailey and Jakicic's timed protocol runs about **31–44 minutes**: set the stage (2 min), review targets and proficiency (2 min), look at data one target at a time and regroup students (10 min), decide instructional responses with each teacher sharing their strategy (10–15 min), pick reassessment items (5–10 min), and note lessons learned (2–5 min) ([Analyzing CFA Data Protocol](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_CTPBPLC/analyzingcommonformativeassessmentdataprotocol.pdf)). The *Taking Action* version also fits 45–60 minutes, but only **if teachers review their own data before the meeting** ([Team Protocol for Analyzing Common Assessment Data](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_TARTI2nd/teamprotocolforanalyzingcommonassessmentdata.pdf)). Another form states plainly: "Each teacher must fill in his or her data prior to the meeting" ([Sample Data Analysis Protocol](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_HLPLCSI/sampledataanalysisprotocol.pdf)).

Picking up where you left off is built into the official agenda. "Focus it" opens with **"Where are we in the PDSA cycle?"**, and "Review it" closes with owners, the next meeting's agenda, and the data to bring ([Team Meeting Agenda Template](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_CTPBPLC/teammeetingagendatemplate.pdf)). WestEd's VITAL tool likewise ends each meeting with "Outline an agenda for the team's next meeting" ([WestEd VITAL tool](https://vital.wested.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/VITAL_Tool_PLC-Scheduling-and-Agenda-Tool.docx)). Teachers suggest putting pacing and calendar events in before the meeting because it "saves valuable meeting time" ([Edutopia](https://www.edutopia.org/article/overcoming-interpersonal-challenges-in-plcs)). Good minutes are brief and decision-focused: one consultant notes the recorder's job is to make sure "owners/dates are captured before the meeting ends" ([Solved Consulting](https://www.solvedconsulting.com/blog/plc-agenda-templates-30-45-60-minutes-doc)).

The unit planning tools agree on the order of work. Name the standard, define the end-of-unit evidence and proficiency, unwrap into targets, choose which targets the CFA will check, and put the CFA and data-meeting dates on a calendar before teaching ([Five-Step Unit-Planning Template](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_CTPBPLC/five-stepunit-planningtemplate.pdf); [Taking Action Unit Assessment Plan](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_TARTI2nd/unitassessmentplan.pdf)). **No published source gives a standard meeting-by-meeting map** for a three- or four-week cycle. The meeting mapping recommended below is therefore an inference from the timed protocols, not an official Solution Tree sequence.

## Comparing classrooms is the point, so protect it with norms and privacy defaults

DuFour is explicit that comparing results across teachers is the engine of the model, not a side effect. Most teachers "can teach an entire career and not know if they teach a particular concept more or less effectively than the teacher next door." When teachers see that "similar students taught by their colleagues have demonstrated proficiency on the same assessment, they are open to exploring new practices" ([DuFour, DuFour & Eaker](https://allthingsplc.info/the-case-for-common-formative-assessments/)). The templates turn this into simple prompts: "What strategies did teammates use whose students performed well?" ([Data Analysis Protocol](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_RTESSA/data-analysis-protocol.pdf)) and a three-column table of **Teachers | Strategies Used | Overall Pass Rate** ([Sample Data Analysis Protocol](https://cloudfront-s3.solutiontree.com/pdfs/Reproducibles_HLPLCSI/sampledataanalysisprotocol.pdf)). Practical ways to share include giving colleagues lesson plans, inviting them to observe, or videotaping a lesson ([ASCD](https://www.ascd.org/el/articles/how-plcs-do-data-right)). One protocol has the teacher with the highest results reteach a mixed group of students, and insists on "factual data—no interpretations" ([Leiva handout](https://solutiontree.s3.us-west-2.amazonaws.com/solutiontree.com/media/pdf/event-materials/st-charles-plc-23/Leiva_FromDataMeetings_(HO)_36-lorez.pdf)). Invitation-based observation and PLC+ learning walks are designed so teachers can learn from each other "without feeling judged" ([Corwin PLC+](https://www.corwin.com/docs/default-source/resources-documents/us-1532187-plc-learning-walks.pdf?sfvrsn=96236a58_0)).

The risk is real. A district handbook warns that common assessments can create anxiety among teachers who fear "the results might be seen as a judgment of them" ([Mount Vernon CSD Handbook](https://www.nysed.gov/sites/default/files/STLE%20PLC%20Handbook%20MVCSD.pdf)). An implementation map found many teachers "reluctant to share individual teacher results" and preferring aggregate views ([Central Rivers AEA](https://prevmain.centralriversaea.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/M11_PLC-Focusing-on-Results-Part-II-IC-Map-Revised-6.2.17.pdf)). One school rejected the DuFour model after it was used to identify who taught a concept "best" ([Alfie Kohn](https://www.alfiekohn.org/?p=1809)), though that is a single anecdote. DuFour-based guidance asks leaders to commit that common assessment data "will not be used in a punitive way to formally or informally evaluate teacher performance" ([PLCs and Data](https://www.scribd.com/document/441100774/PLCs-and-Data-pdf)). That quote comes from a secondary source and should be checked against *Learning by Doing*. The mitigations that recur are setting data norms, reviewing them at the start of each data meeting, and framing the question as "which approach worked" rather than "which teacher" ([Edutopia](https://www.edutopia.org/article/overcoming-interpersonal-challenges-in-plcs)).

On student privacy, FERPA generally allows teachers to see student records without parental consent when they have a "legitimate educational interest," as their district defines it in its annual notice. Schools must also use "reasonable methods" to limit each official to the records they need ([studentprivacy.ed.gov FAQ](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/faq/who-school-official-under-ferpa); [CRS IF13155](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IF13155.html)). **No federal guidance addresses PLCs specifically.** Teaching the same course with a common assessment, or sharing intervention groups, is the usual justification, but the answer depends on local policy. Existing tools handle this poorly. Microsoft Teams' PLC notebook gives every member full access to every section, with no private area ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/training/modules/collaborate-colleagues-staff-plc-teams-microsoft-teams/modify-plc-team-learner-concerns)). Public reporting rules suppress groups smaller than about 5 or 10 students ([CT EdSight](https://edsight.ct.gov/relatedreports/BDCRE%20Data%20Suppression%20Rules.pdf); [DC OSSE](https://osse.dc.gov/sites/default/files/dc/sites/osse/publication/attachments/OSSE%20Student%20Privacy%20and%20Data%20Suppression%20Policy_FINAL_1.27.22.pdf)). DC also has a separate policy protecting *teacher-level* data ([OSSE workforce policy](https://osse.dc.gov/sites/default/files/dc/sites/osse/publication/attachments/OSSE%20Educational%20Workforce%20Privacy%20and%20Data%20Suppression%20Policy%20FINAL%201.27.22.pdf)). Those thresholds were written for public reports, so applying them inside a team is prudent practice, not a legal requirement. Sensible defaults for PLC Launchpad:

- Each teacher sees full names only for their own students; teammates see initials or IDs.
- Results are shown by target first and by classroom second, labeled "Class A/B/C" unless a teacher chooses to reveal their own.
- Sign-in is limited to the school's Google domain, and data is stored in school-owned Drive.
- Nothing is visible to administrators or connected to evaluation by default.

## Today's tools handle data or documents, but none guides the whole loop

The tools market splits into four groups ([Solution Tree Global PD Teams](https://www.solutiontree.com/globalpdteams/best-practices); [MasteryConnect on G2](https://g2.com/products/masteryconnect/reviews); [Schoolytics](https://info.schoolytics.com/plc-meeting-template); [Microsoft PLC team](https://support.microsoft.com/en-US/education/create-a-plc-team-in-microsoft-teams); [Core Collaborative](https://thecorecollaborative.com/?p=7156)):

- **Professional development content platforms**, such as Solution Tree's Global PD Teams.
- **Assessment and data platforms with some PLC features**, such as MasteryConnect, Eduphoria, Illuminate, Otus, Formative and Schoolytics.
- **Blank collaboration spaces with PLC templates**, such as Microsoft Teams with OneNote, Google Docs and Sheets, and TpT binders.
- **New AI tools**, such as The Core Collaborative's AI-PLC Agent.

What the data platforms do well is automatic, standards-aligned results and reteach lists. What they do poorly is usability. Reviewers call MasteryConnect "quite confusing at times," note that its red/yellow/green status reflects only the *last* assessment, and one teacher refused to bring it into a PLC because the shared items were low-quality "fill-in-the-bubble" tests ([G2](https://g2.com/products/masteryconnect/reviews); [Common Sense Education](https://www.commonsense.org/education/reviews/masteryconnect/teacher-reviews/5033390)). Vendors' own pitches name the time teachers lose "opening systems and filtering spreadsheets" before meetings ([Progress Learning](https://progresslearning.com/news-blog/establishing-data-analysis-routines/)). Microsoft's notebook follows DuFour's templates but is a static notebook with no guided workflow ([Microsoft Learn](https://docs.microsoft.com/en-gb/learn/modules/collaborate-colleagues-live-teams-meetings-onenote/collaborate-peers-plc-notebooks)). Eduphoria puts PLC collaboration in the same product as staff evaluation ([Zoftwarehub](https://zoftwarehub.com/en-bh/products/eduphoria/overview)), which is exactly the pairing that makes teachers wary of comparison. A Kappan article sums up the deeper gap: PLCs see "an abundance of information about effects and few clues about causes," because student results are rarely linked to the teaching behind them ([Kappan](https://kappanonline.org/?p=123502)).

The research found **no tool that walks a team through the whole cycle step by step, no tool with invitation-based or anonymous strategy sharing tied to results, and no guided PLC app built on Google**. Google-based options are templates or Classroom analytics ([Schoolytics on Google Cloud](https://cloud.google.com/customers/schoolytics)). These findings come from searches, and not finding a tool is not proof that none exists. Still, the consistent picture is that the planning and response half of the cycle lives in scattered Docs, while the data half lives in district platforms the team does not control.

## Recommended minimal flow: one standard, eight steps, four meetings

The core plan has eight steps. Each one maps directly onto the four questions and onto fields that recur across the published templates. Everything in the "Optional extras" row of the earlier table stays hidden unless a team turns it on.

| # | Step (critical question) | Fields the team must complete | Meeting |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | **Team setup** (first time only) | Team name and members (from Google accounts); 3–5 norms, with one pre-written data norm to accept or edit ("We look at results to learn, not to judge; student names stay in this room") | Meeting 1, first 5–10 min |
| 1 | **Pick one essential standard** (Q1) | Standard text in student-friendly words, chosen from the team's list or typed in; why it matters is optional | Meeting 1 |
| 2 | **Write 2–4 "I can" targets** (Q1) | Target text in "I can" form; prerequisites and vocabulary optional | Meeting 1 |
| 3 | **Agree on proficient** (Q2) | For each target, a cut ("2 of 3 correct" or "level 3 on rubric") and an optional example of proficient work | Meeting 1 or 2 |
| 4 | **Plan the CFA and goal** (Q2) | Which targets the CFA checks; a link to the CFA (Google Form or Doc); give-by date; data-meeting date; one-sentence goal pre-filled as "At least __% of our students will be proficient on [target] by [date]" | Meeting 2 |
| — | *Teach and give the CFA; each teacher enters or imports results before Meeting 3* | Score by student by target (Forms or Sheets import) | Between meetings |
| 5 | **Look at results** (Q2) | App-built view by target, then by class (Class A/B/C); one line on error patterns; each teacher's strategy in a sentence | Meeting 3, about 25 min |
| 6 | **Plan the response** (Q3, Q4) | Three groups (more time / on target / extend), filled automatically from the cut; for each group: plan, who leads, when; recheck date and items | Meeting 3, about 15 min |
| 7 | **Recheck and reflect** (Q3) | Percent proficient after the response; did we meet the goal; "What will we keep or change, and did our teaching change?" | Meeting 4, about 15–20 min, then on to the next standard |

This is the *Essential Learning Target Plan* expanded just enough to include norms and a link to the CFA. It matches Solution Tree's "one team, one standard, one cycle" starting advice. Meeting 3 follows the timed Bailey and Jakicic data protocol, and Meeting 4 leaves time to begin the next plan. Each meeting opens with an automatic "where we are" summary and closes with next steps (owner and date), which carry into the next agenda. That replaces narrative minutes entirely.

## Conclusion

The earlier version felt overwhelming because "complete" was defined by the *forms*. The PLC model defines complete by the *questions*. A plan is complete when one essential standard has targets and a proficiency bar (Q1), a CFA with results (Q2), a plan for the students who need more (Q3), and a plan for the students who are ready to go further (Q4). Anything that does not move a team from one of those answers to the next belongs in an optional drawer. The research also suggests the hardest part is not the planning but the moment after the data, when teams must compare results without anyone feeling judged. That moment is where the most valuable PLC behavior lives, and it is also where existing tools offer least.

PLC Launchpad can therefore stand out without adding anything. It can do fewer steps than the forms, carry the team's progress from one 45-minute meeting to the next automatically, and handle the sensitive comparison step better than any current tool, with privacy defaults and invitation-based sharing. The biggest open question is not technical. It is whether teachers at a given school trust that results stay inside the team. A short data norm, accepted at setup and shown at every data meeting, is the cheapest feature the app can ship and probably the one that matters most.
